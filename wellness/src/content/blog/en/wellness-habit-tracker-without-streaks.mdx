A useful habit record tells you what happened and what to try next. A missed day should remain visible as information; it does not need to erase everything you already recorded. Start with one action, a few planned opportunities and a short note about each attempt.

This is a planning worksheet. It does not diagnose a condition, measure fitness or promise that a routine will become automatic after a particular number of days. You can use a notebook or an ordinary notes app you already own.

## Choose an action you can actually observe

“Be healthier” is too broad to record. “Put tomorrow's walking shoes by the door after dinner” is a specific action with a clear time and place. Choose something useful to you, rather than copying somebody else's daily target.

Write three lines before making a table:

- **The action:** What, exactly, will I do?
- **The opportunity:** When and where could it reasonably happen?
- **The review:** When will I look back and decide what to change?

For an activity plan, the [weekly movement worksheet](/weekly-movement-planning-worksheet/) helps you find realistic calendar slots. Keep prescribed medicines, treatments and safety instructions separate: this flexible worksheet is not a reason to change a clinician's directions.

## Record the outcome, not a judgment

Use plain labels that preserve what happened:

| Label | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Done | I completed the action I planned |
| Changed | I did a different or shorter version; I wrote down what |
| Missed | I planned an opportunity but did not do the action |
| Not planned | There was no planned attempt that day |

“Changed” is not automatically equivalent to “Done.” Writing the actual action prevents a smaller version from being mistaken for the original target. A planned rest day is also different from a missed attempt.

## Copy this small tracker

```text
Week beginning:
One action I chose:
Planned opportunities:
Why this matters to me:

Date / opportunity:
Outcome: done / changed / missed / not planned
What I actually did:
What helped or got in the way:

At the weekly review:
One thing to keep:
One thing to adjust:
Next planned opportunity:
```

Repeat the four daily lines only as often as you find useful. Avoid adding food, weight, sleep or symptom scores merely because an app has fields for them. Keep private notes somewhere you control.

## A worked example: preparing for a walk

This is a hypothetical week, not a reader result or a study. The action is **put shoes and a jacket by the door after dinner on Monday, Wednesday and Friday**. It is preparation for an existing walking plan, not a claim that arranging clothes fulfils an activity recommendation.

| Opportunity | What happened | Useful note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Done | Preparing before washing up was easy |
| Wednesday | Missed | Dinner happened away from home |
| Friday | Changed | Put shoes out; jacket was still in the car |
| Other days | Not planned | No daily target was set |

The record contains one completed action, one changed action and one missed opportunity. It does not show a seven-day failure, and it does not show three completed walks. At the review, the useful adjustment might be to prepare before leaving home on evenings out.

## Review the obstacle before changing the goal

The [NIDDK guide to changing habits](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diet-nutrition/changing-habits-better-health) recommends recording progress, reviewing goals and planning around setbacks. It also explains that a setback does not mean the whole effort has failed. Our worksheet is one way to organise those observations; it has not been tested against streak-based apps.

At the end of the week, ask whether the cue, location or preparation caused the difficulty. Change one thing so you can see whether it helps. For example, move the reminder earlier instead of adding five new habits at once.

If the action is no longer useful or practical, revise it deliberately. You do not owe the tracker a longer streak. For a broader reflection, the [weekly review of food, movement and rest](/simple-wellness-weekly-review/) can help you choose what deserves attention next.

## Keep the record proportionate

If logging makes you anxious, consumes more effort than the activity, or encourages you to ignore pain, illness or professional advice, step back from the tracker. A few notes at the end of a week may be sufficient. This tool should help you make a practical decision, not become another obligation.
